# The Box, paired and used over TCP (2026-11-10)

**Question.** Does a Box that starts with nothing offer nothing to a
stranger, pair from an invitation typed into it and a welcome made by the Key
app, follow new settings from the owner, keep its key and its members across
a restart, refuse a second process on its directory, turn itself off when
the settings drop it, and refuse a mistyped or a different invitation
(`docs/BOX-RULES.md` sections 1 to 3 and 5)? And, with a POS connector, does
it refuse a token file others can read, match the counters' paid notices to
the POS and tell the owner, refuse a notice from a device that is not a
counter, close an order once across a restart, close none for a sale never
paid, and run none of it while unpaired (sections 4 and 5a, BX-40 to BX-43)?

**Answer.** Yes, in the run made after the script's last edit (2026-11-10,
rerun in full with the POS steps added): all fourteen steps passed. Steps 10
to 14 were added on 2026-11-10 for the POS on the Box.

## Method

`scripts/box-e2e.sh` runs the whole check and prints PASS, FAIL or NOT RUN
for each numbered step. No Bitcoin Core is involved. The pieces:

- The Box is the `openbmt-box` binary, started on a fresh directory under
  `~/.cache/openbmt/` that is removed on every exit. Its standard input is a
  fifo the script holds open, so that the console commands `invite`, `state`
  and `members` can be sent and read back from its log.
- The Key app and the owner phone are the `box_pair` example of
  `openbmt-box`. It uses the core functions (`openbmt_core::boxpair`,
  `Settings`, `SignedSettings`) and the link's client. It makes the
  invitation, fetches the claim with the client and checks it against the
  invitation, builds the first settings (two counters and the Box listed as a
  Box, signed by both roots), makes the welcome and sends it, and plays the
  owner and the counters when they make mailboxes and pass sealed messages.
  Its keys come from fixed test names: test only, never use.
- The script carries nothing between the two. The invitation is typed into
  the Box's console; the claim, the welcome, the settings and the messages
  all go through the Box's TCP port on 127.0.0.1. The Box's own key is made by
  the Box from the system's randomness, and the Key app side learns it only
  from the claim.
- Steps 10 to 14 need a Box with a POS connector, which the `openbmt-box`
  binary does not carry (a connector is added by the program that links the
  library). They run `box_pair pos-box`, which is the same `BoxRunner` with a
  connector made of files in a directory; the line format is in the example's
  header. The counters' notices are sent by `box_pair notice` (a counter key
  seals a paid notice to the Box through its TCP port) and the owner reads
  the Box's POS notices with `box_pair posrecv`. The test connector finds the
  one open order of the sale's amount and, when told it can, closes it and
  writes each call to a file the script counts.

| 10 | A pos-box is paired. A `pos.token` at mode 0644 is refused by name (BX-41, "can be read by its group or by others (mode 0644)"); the relay still makes a mailbox, and the connector was given no token. | 11 | Counter 1 sends a notice that sale 1 opened and one that it is paid. The POS holds a closed order with a Bitcoin tender for the amount. The Box takes both (2 taken, 0 refused), matches them, and the owner opens a POS notice, signature good, "order o1 tender t-o1 matches sale 1". The Box is stopped and started, a final notice for the sale is sent, and the connector's call count is still one. | PASS |

## What is NOT covered

- One machine, loopback only. No network between the devices, no packet loss,
  no Tor. The Box reached over an onion service is the link's route and is
  tested there (LK-31).
- The Key app and the owner phone are an example program, not the real apps.
  What their screens say (BX-14, BX-17), the Key app's own 30-minute expiry
  and its one open invitation at a time (BX-11) are not tested here: the
  example checks the claim and spends its invitation, nothing more.
- The Box's own 30-minute expiry (BX-12) is tested in the crate's unit tests
  with a clock the test moves, not here: the script would have to wait.
- A crash between the writes of a welcome (BX-15) and a full disk are tested
  with storage that fails from the n-th write on, in the crate's unit tests,
  not by killing the process.
- What a stranger on the network can still do to a Box with an invitation
  open: say hello, ask for the claim (which proves nothing without the
  invitation) and send welcomes, which are refused and change nothing unless
  their MAC checks. Step 9 shows two refused welcomes and the real pairing
  after them; the unit tests send a thousand and show the rate of one a
  second.
- The POS side is tested here with a connector made of files, not a vendor's
  API. What a vendor's connector says on the wire, its rate limits and its
  errors are the connector's own tests; the unit tests of the Box cover a
  connector that fails, times out or returns garbage.
- A non-counter's notice is shown here by the relay refusing a sender that is
  not on the list (step 11). A device that is on the list but is not a
  counter (the Box's own role check, BX-40) is covered by the unit tests; the
  link seals a paid notice only from a counter, so the example cannot make one.
- The Box's 30-second timer is not waited for here: the readings in steps 10
  to 14 are the ones a notice sets off at once. The timer is tested with a
  clock the test moves.
- The Box notes a message of any kind other than Settings and the paid
  notice as not handled yet.
- No independent security audit has been done. The Box's files are mode 0600
  and the invitation travels only on the merchant's console, but the
  computer it is typed on is trusted, as the rules say.
