# When someone else holds the money

Money in a bank or a payment company is not in the owner's hands. Someone
else decides when the owner can have it. Most days they pay on time, and
most shops never meet anything on this page. The cases below are the days
they did not. In each one, people and businesses could not get their own
money out, through no fault of their own. All of them are on the public
record.

The worst come first: money that never fully came back. Then come cases
where the money was held for months or years, and last, fines and
settlements over money that was held. Every case says who held the money,
what they did with it, and how it ended. Where a figure is worked out here
from published ones, the page says so.

Bitcoin in the shop's own wallet takes that party out of the middle. It
does not take away the shop's own duties: the keys and their backups are
then the shop's to keep, as [Who controls the money](safety.md) explains.

## Money that never fully came back

**Lebanon, since 2019.** In 2019 Lebanon's banks stopped letting people
take out their own dollars. In March 2021, 1.45 million depositors had
$107 billion in the banks that they could not freely take out. What they
could take from a dollar account was paid in Lebanese pounds at a rate the
central bank set, so each withdrawal lost 70% or more of its value against
the market rate. In 2025 most customers could still take out only about
$500 a month.

> "No bank transfers are allowed, so we cannot import materials."
>
> A manufacturer in Lebanon, November 2019.[^q-lebanon]

**How it ended:** it has not. A draft law of December 2025 would repay up
to $100,000 per depositor in cash over four years and the rest in bonds.
Parliament had not passed it.[^lebanon]

**Cyprus, 2013.** Overnight, to rescue its two largest banks, the
government of Cyprus made the account holders pay for it. Nobody asked
them. At Laiki Bank, every balance above €100,000, about €4 billion in
all, was taken to cover the bank's losses. At Bank of Cyprus, about 20,000
account holders had €3.8 billion above that limit, and 47.5% of it was
turned into shares of the bank.

> "We can't make any withdrawals, we can't make deposits, pay our suppliers."
>
> A florist in the old town of Nicosia, with the banks shut, March 2013.[^q-cyprus]

**How it ended:** by 2019, Laiki's account holders had got back about 6
cents of each euro taken. A business with €500,000 there kept €100,000,
and of the €400,000 taken, got back about €24,000 (worked out here). In
September 2025, twelve years on, €44.36 million of compensation was paid
to 4,524 depositors, about €9,800 each (worked out here).[^cyprus]

**TMON and WeMakePrice, South Korea, 2024.** Two large shopping websites
took customers' payments and paid their sellers later. In July 2024, in
what the press called a massive cash crunch, they stopped paying. About
48,000 sellers were cut off from about 1.2 trillion won, about $900
million: money for goods they had already sold. That is about $19,000 per
seller (worked out here). Both companies went to the bankruptcy court on
29 July 2024.

> "If the settlement is not done by then, it means bankruptcy."
>
> A seller of household goods in Seoul, owed about 200 million won of May sales, July 2024 (translated).[^q-tmon]

**How it ended:** at the last report, in September 2024, the sellers were
still unpaid, and how much they would get back was left to a court plan
due that December. The government's answer was loans and credit
guarantees: credit against the sellers' own unpaid money.[^tmon]

**Synapse, United States, 2024.** Synapse moved money between finance
apps and the banks that held it. When it went bankrupt in April 2024, its
records and the banks' records did not agree, and customers' accounts were
frozen for months. Mainvest, which raised money from the public for
restaurants and small shops, could not reach about $2.4 million and closed
in June 2024.

> "A bank just robbed us."
>
> A savings-app customer in Tampa, Florida, who had about $94,000 there and was shown $128.68 back, November 2024.[^q-synapse]

**How it ended:** one app's 13,725 customers had $64.9 million in their
accounts and were offered $11.8 million back, about 18 cents on the dollar
(worked out here). Up to $96 million was missing in all. Deposit insurance
did not cover any of it, because Synapse was not a bank.[^synapse]

**Digital River, United States and Europe, 2024 to 2025.** Digital River
sold software and other digital products for the companies that made
them, took the customers' payments, and paid each maker its share. From
July 2024 it stopped paying many of them for sales they had already made.
Its lawyers said it disputed the debts.

> "We've experienced a complete halt in software sales revenue payments."
>
> The managing director of Traction Software, a UK software seller, October 2024.[^q-digitalriver]

**How it ended:** its German companies filed for insolvency in January
2025, and its US marketing company filed for liquidation in 2025 with
about $45.2 million of secured debt and less than $50,000 of assets. No
payout to the unpaid sellers had been reported.[^digitalriver]

**CharityGiving, United Kingdom, 2013 to 2014.** The CharityGiving website
collected donations for charities and passed them on. In July 2013 it was
suspended over how its trustees ran the money. It owed £1.68 million to
1,812 charities and good causes, and had £709,529 left in its accounts.

**How it ended:** in July 2014, a year later, the High Court ruled that
what was left be shared out. Each charity got about 33p of every pound
given for it.[^dovetrust]

**Zimbabwe, 2019.** In February 2019, by government order, the US dollars
in people's bank accounts were renamed RTGS dollars. They then traded at
2.5 to one US dollar, so a $1,000 balance was worth about $400 (worked out
here).

**How it ended:** in 2022 the government said it had begun compensating
balances of $1,000 or less. It did not publish how much.[^zimbabwe]

**Argentina, 2001 to 2003.** In December 2001, to stop people emptying the
banks, the government froze bank deposits. Dollar savings were then turned
into pesos by order. In early 2002, 27,700 million pesos were locked into
fixed terms.

**How it ended:** in February 2003 nearly half of it, 12,995 million
pesos, was still locked. The government later paid part of the difference
between the dollars and the pesos in bonds.[^argentina]

## Money held for months or years

**Imperial Bank, Kenya, 2015.** The central bank placed Imperial Bank in
receivership on 13 October 2015, with its customers' money inside, and
they were paid back in stages. Five years on, a payment in September 2020
brought the total returned to 37.3% of the deposits.

**How it ended:** about 45,700 of some 50,000 depositors had been paid in
full by 2021. The other 4,300 or so were still waiting. Payments to them
began in April 2023, seven and a half years after the bank
closed.[^imperial]

**Flipcause, United States, Stripe, 2025.** Flipcause collected online
donations for charities and schools. On 2 December 2025, after a warning
from a card network, Stripe held all of Flipcause's funds, and two days
later ended its service. Flipcause filed for bankruptcy on 19 December
owing more than $29 million to over 3,200 organisations, about $9,000
each (worked out here). One of them, Millenium School, was owed
$54,368.20.

> "It's a constant fight to get the money. I have to fight for each transfer."
>
> The executive director of East Oakland Collective, which runs food pantries, while Flipcause held more than $100,000 of its donations, September 2025.[^q-flipcause]

**How it ended:** not settled at the last report. Flipcause's own handling
of the money is also under question.[^flipcause]

**Brazil, 1990.** In March 1990, to fight inflation, the new government
froze about 70% of all the money in the country, $115 billion, for 18
months. Anything above about $600 in a savings account was locked until
September 1991, and companies struggled to pay their staff.

**How it ended:** the money stayed frozen for the 18 months. The source
does not say how much of its value it kept.[^brazil]

**Nigeria, 2023.** When new banknotes came in from December 2022, the cash
in circulation fell from about ₦3.3 trillion to ₦982 billion within four
months, and at first a company could take only ₦500,000 a week out of its
own account. Nigerian Breweries, which took about 80% of its retail sales
in cash, reported sales down 29.7% from a year earlier. Poultry farmers
lost more than ₦30 billion of eggs they could not sell.

> "Someone might want to transfer funds to you, but it can't be processed."
>
> A car parts seller in Abuja, February 2023.[^q-nigeria]

**How it ended:** on 3 March 2023 the Supreme Court ruled that the old
notes stayed valid, and the shortage eased.[^nigeria]

**Greece, 2015.** Between November 2014 and the summer of 2015, Greek
businesses took €9.3 billion, 39% of their deposits, out of the banks.
Then the government closed the banks from 28 June to 20 July. A large
payment to a supplier abroad, from a business's own account, needed a
government commission's approval.

> "If we keep going like this we're going to close."
>
> The owner of a bird shop in Athens that imports its stock, July 2015.[^q-greece]

**How it ended:** the banks reopened on 20 July. The national bank's own
review found that firms kept importing and the damage stayed smaller than
feared.[^greece]

**Huel, United Kingdom, PayPal, 2016.** Sales at the food company jumped,
and within a week PayPal was holding about £44,000 of its takings: money
its customers had already paid. PayPal planned to keep it for 90 days
while it checked for fraud.

> "They have no legitimate reason to hold my money and earn interest."
>
> Huel's founder, February 2016.[^q-huel]

**How it ended:** after Huel complained, PayPal changed the hold to a share
of each sale for 30 days. On 1 February 2016, £17,000 had been released
and £27,000 was still held. The same report said other UK start-ups had
waited up to six months for their own money.[^huel]

**United Kingdom, 2024.** Tide blocked a small company's account on 3 July
2024 and closed it in August, with £3,100.79 of the company's money
inside. Barclays blocked another company's account on 5 August 2024 and
held £4,020 of its money. In the regulator's own survey, the banks in the
middle of the range closed between 1% and 6.9% of their business accounts
in a year.

**How it ended:** Tide paid out the first company's money on 18 November,
four and a half months after the block, and the Financial Ombudsman
awarded £300 and interest. Barclays released the second company's money
after six weeks.[^uk]

**Yes Bank, India, 2020.** On 5 March 2020, with Yes Bank failing, the
central bank capped what each customer could take out at Rs 50,000,
whatever was in the account. PhonePe, one of the country's largest payment
apps, ran through the bank, and its merchants' payouts stopped for about a
day.

**How it ended:** the cap stayed for 13 days, until the bank was rescued
on 18 March.[^yesbank]

**Silicon Valley Bank, United States, 2023.** The bank failed on Friday
10 March 2023, holding about $151.6 billion that was above the insured
limit. Roku had $487 million there, about a quarter of its cash. Rippling,
a payroll company, had $545 million of its customers' wages locked in the
bank, and lent nearly $130 million of its own money so that their staff
were paid.

**How it ended:** on Sunday the government decided that every depositor
would be paid in full, and the money was available from Monday 13 March.
All of it came back, because the government chose to step in.[^svb]

## Fines and settlements over held money

**PayPal, United States, 2004 and 2017.** Two class actions said PayPal
froze or held money in accounts without explanation. The first was
settled in 2004 with a fund of $9.25 million. The second was settled in
March 2017 with a fund of $3.2 million and changes to how PayPal explains
holds. About 10.5 million accounts could claim and close to 400,000 did.
A basic claim paid for the time the money was held: from $3 for a hold
of up to 30 days to $440 for more than $10,000 held over 150 days.[^paypal]

**PayPal, Poland, 2024.** The Polish competition authority fined PayPal
about $27 million over terms that let it block users' access to their own
money without clear rules. PayPal could appeal.[^poland]

**First American Payment Systems, United States, 2022 to 2025.** The
Federal Trade Commission said this card processor took money from small
businesses' accounts without their consent and tied them to contracts with
hidden terms and exit fees. The company agreed to return $4.9 million. By
February 2025, cheques totalling more than $2.6 million had gone to 5,588
businesses, about $465 each (worked out here).[^fapss]

## What these cases say

Money held by someone else comes with that party's rules, its failures
and its country's emergencies. In none of these cases had the owners done
anything wrong, and in most of them they had no say in what happened to
their money or in when it came back. When it happens, the owner waits on
someone else's decision.

## Why bitcoin in the shop's own wallet is different

Every case above needed someone between the business and its money: a
processor that could hold a payout, a bank that could close an account or
fail, a government that could cap what came out of the banks. Bitcoin in
the shop's own wallet has no one in that place. It is not a balance in an
account; it sits on a network that no single company or government runs,
and it moves only when the shop's own keys sign for it. Nobody can put a
reserve on it, close the account or cap the withdrawals, because there is
no account, and no processor can hold a payout, because there is no
payout.

This holds only while the bitcoin stays in the shop's own wallet. Bitcoin
left with an exchange or a payment company is again money held by someone
else, and changing bitcoin into local money brings a bank back in at that
moment.

## Why the keys need so much care

With nobody in the middle, nobody can undo a mistake either. There is no
fraud desk, no password reset and no court that can move the coins back.
Whoever has the shop's 12 words can spend its money, and if every copy of
the words is lost, the money stays where it is for good.

That is why OpenBMT asks for more care at setup than a bank account does.
The words are made where nobody else can see them, written by hand, never
typed into a phone or a connected computer, and put on steel as soon as
possible.

## Why a coin or a die

Every wallet starts from one large random number, and the 12 words are
that number written out. Phones, computers and hardware wallets make the
number with chips and software, and those have had faults before: a number
that looked random was one that someone who knew the fault could work out.

A coin or a die has no chip, no software and no update. Nothing can reach
into it over a network, and if it favours one side, the method in
[Setting up a shop](setting-up.md#why-so-many-flips-and-rolls) removes the
bias. What remains is the computer that turns the flips or rolls into
words, and OpenBMT keeps that step as small and as checkable as it can.
The key maker page runs on Tails with the network off, shows its working,
and gives commands to check the result by hand, and Electrum, made by
other people, must show the same wallet before the shop uses it.

> funds.**
