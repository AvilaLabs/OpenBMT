# When someone else holds the money

Most days, a bank or a payment company pays a shop's money out on time,
and most shops never meet anything on this page. The cases below are the
exceptions, and they are all on the public record. In each one, someone
else held the money, and that party, or a government, decided when the
business could have it, or whether it got it back at all.

The worst cases come first: money that never fully came back. Then come
cases where the money was held for months or years, and last, fines and
settlements over money that was held. Every case says who held the money,
why, and how it ended. Where a figure is worked out here from two
published ones, the page says so.

Bitcoin in the shop's own wallet takes that party out of the middle. It
does not take away the shop's own duties: the keys and their backups are
then the shop's to keep, as [Who controls the money](safety.md) explains.

## Money that never fully came back

**Lebanon, since 2019.** In the financial crisis that began in 2019,
Lebanon's banks stopped letting depositors freely take out their money.
In March 2021, 1.45 million depositors had $107 billion in the banks that
they could not freely take out. A dollar account paid out in Lebanese
pounds at a rate set by the central bank, so a withdrawal lost 70% or
more of its value against the market rate.

**How it ended:** it has not. In 2025 most customers could still take out
only about $500 a month. A draft law of December 2025 would repay up to
$100,000 per depositor in cash over four years and the rest in bonds;
parliament had not passed it.[^lebanon]

**Cyprus, 2013.** To rescue its two largest banks, Cyprus took the losses
from deposits above €100,000. At Laiki Bank, about €4 billion above that
limit was lost. At Bank of Cyprus, about 20,000 depositors had €3.8 billion
above the limit, and 47.5% of it was turned into bank shares.

**How it ended:** by 2019, Laiki's depositors had recovered about 6 cents of
each euro above the limit. In September 2025, twelve years on, €44.36
million of compensation was paid to 4,524 depositors, about €9,800 each
(worked out here).[^cyprus]

**TMON and WeMakePrice, South Korea, 2024.** The two online marketplaces
collected the money for their sellers' sales and paid it out to the
sellers later. In July 2024, in what the press called a massive cash
crunch, they stopped paying. The government counted about 48,000 sellers
owed about 1.2 trillion won, about $900 million. Both companies applied to
the bankruptcy court on 29 July 2024.

**How it ended:** at the last report, in September 2024, the sellers were
still unpaid, and how much they would get back was left to a court plan
due that December. The government arranged loans and credit guarantees for
the sellers to keep going. That was borrowed money, not their own money
back.[^tmon]

**Synapse, United States, 2024.** Synapse sat between apps and the banks
that held their customers' money. When it went bankrupt in April 2024,
its records and the banks' records did not agree, and accounts were
frozen for months. Mainvest, which raised money from the public for
restaurants and small shops, could not reach about $2.4 million and closed
in June 2024.

**How it ended:** one app's 13,725 customers had $64.9 million deposited and
were offered $11.8 million back, about 18 cents on the dollar (worked out
here). Up to $96 million was missing in all, and deposit insurance did not
apply, because Synapse was not a bank.[^synapse]

**Digital River, United States and Europe, 2024 to 2025.** Digital River
sold software and other digital products for the companies that made them,
and paid each its share. From July 2024 it stopped paying many of them.
Its lawyers said it disputed the debts.

**How it ended:** its German companies filed for insolvency in January
2025, and in 2025 its US marketing company filed for liquidation under
Chapter 7 with about $45.2 million of secured debt and less than $50,000
of assets. No payout to the unpaid sellers had been reported.[^digitalriver]

**CharityGiving, United Kingdom, 2013 to 2014.** The website ran
fundraising pages and passed the donations on to charities. It was
suspended in July 2013 over how its trustees ran the donations. It owed
£1.68 million to 1,812 charities and good causes and had £709,529 in its
bank accounts.

**How it ended:** in July 2014 the High Court ruled that the money left be
shared out: each charity would get about 33p for every pound it was
owed.[^dovetrust]

**Zimbabwe, 2019.** In February 2019, US dollars in bank accounts were
renamed RTGS dollars by government order, and then traded at 2.5 to one US
dollar, so a $1,000 balance was worth about $400 (worked out here).

**How it ended:** in 2022 the government said it had begun compensating
balances of $1,000 or less. The amount was not published.[^zimbabwe]

**Argentina, 2001 to 2003.** To stop a run on the banks, deposits were
frozen in December 2001, and dollar deposits were converted into pesos by
order. In early 2002, 27,700 million pesos were locked into fixed terms.

**How it ended:** in February 2003 nearly half of it, 12,995 million pesos,
was still locked. The government later paid part of the difference
between the dollars and the pesos in bonds.[^argentina]

## Money held for months or years

**Imperial Bank, Kenya, 2015.** The central bank placed Imperial Bank in
receivership on 13 October 2015, and its depositors were paid back in
stages. A payment in September 2020, five years on, brought the total
recovered to 37.3% of the deposits.

**How it ended:** most depositors, about 45,700 of some 50,000, had been
paid in full by 2021. The other 4,300 or so were still waiting, and
payments to them began in April 2023, seven and a half years after the
bank closed.[^imperial]

**Flipcause, United States, Stripe, 2025.** Flipcause ran online donations
for charities and schools. On 2 December 2025, after a warning from a card
network, Stripe held all of its funds, and two days later ended its
service. Flipcause filed for bankruptcy on 19 December owing more than
$29 million to over 3,200 organisations, about $9,000 each on average
(worked out here). One of them, Millenium School, was owed $54,368.20.

**How it ended:** not settled at the last report. The platform's own
handling of the money is also under question.[^flipcause]

**Brazil, 1990.** To fight inflation, a government plan moved $115 billion,
about 70% of the money in the country, into the central bank for 18
months. Anything above about $600 in a savings account was frozen until
September 1991, and companies struggled to pay their staff.

**How it ended:** the money stayed frozen for the 18 months. The source
does not say how much of its value it kept.[^brazil]

**Nigeria, 2023.** When new banknotes came in from December 2022, the cash
in circulation fell from about ₦3.3 trillion to ₦982 billion within four
months, and a company could withdraw only ₦500,000 a week at first.
Nigerian Breweries, which took about 80% of its retail sales in cash,
reported sales down 29.7% from a year earlier. Poultry farmers lost more
than ₦30 billion of eggs they could not sell.

**How it ended:** the Supreme Court ruled on 3 March 2023 that the old notes
stayed valid, and the shortage eased.[^nigeria]

**Greece, 2015.** Greek businesses took €9.3 billion, 39% of their deposits, out of the banks
between November 2014 and the summer of 2015. The banks then closed from
28 June to 20 July, and a large payment to a supplier abroad needed a
government commission's approval.

**How it ended:** the banks reopened on 20 July. The national bank's own
review found that firms kept importing and the damage stayed smaller than
feared.[^greece]

**Huel, United Kingdom, PayPal, 2016.** Sales at the food company jumped,
and within a week PayPal was holding about £44,000 of its takings, to be
kept for 90 days while PayPal checked for fraud.

**How it ended:** after Huel complained, PayPal changed the hold to a share
of each sale for 30 days. On 1 February 2016, £17,000 had been released
and £27,000 was still held. The same report said other UK start-ups had
waited up to six months for their money.[^huel]

**United Kingdom, 2024.** A small limited company's account at Tide was
blocked on 3 July 2024 and closed in August. Barclays blocked another
company's account on 5 August 2024 and held £4,020. In the regulator's own
survey, the banks in the middle of the range closed between 1% and 6.9% of
their business accounts in a year.

**How it ended:** Tide paid out the first company's £3,100.79 on 18
November, four and a half months after the block, and the Financial
Ombudsman awarded £300 and interest. Barclays released the second
company's money after six weeks.[^uk]

**Yes Bank, India, 2020.** On 5 March 2020, with the bank failing, the
central bank capped what each depositor could take out at Rs 50,000.
Depositors had already taken out Rs 72,000 crore in the two months before.
PhonePe, one of the country's largest payment apps, ran through the bank,
and its merchants' payouts stopped for about a day.

**How it ended:** the cap stayed for 13 days, until the bank was rescued on
18 March.[^yesbank]

**Silicon Valley Bank, United States, 2023.** The bank closed on Friday
10 March 2023, holding about $151.6 billion that was above the insured
limit. Roku had $487 million there, about a quarter of its cash. Rocket
Lab had $38 million. Rippling, a payroll company, had $545 million of its
customers' wages locked in the bank, and lent nearly $130 million of its
own money so that their staff were paid.

**How it ended:** on Sunday the government decided that every depositor
would be paid in full, and the money was available from Monday 13 March.
All of it came back.[^svb]

## Fines and settlements over held money

**PayPal, United States, 2004 and 2017.** Two class actions said PayPal
froze or held money in accounts without explanation. The first was
settled in 2004 with a fund of $9.25 million. The second was settled in
March 2017 with a fund of $3.2 million and changes to how PayPal explains
holds. About 10.5 million accounts could claim and close to 400,000 did.
A basic claim paid for the time the money was held: from $3 for a hold
of up to 30 days to $440 for more than $10,000 held over 150 days.[^paypal]

**PayPal, Poland, 2024.** The Polish competition authority fined PayPal
about $27 million over terms that let it block users' access to their
money without clear rules. PayPal could appeal.[^poland]

**First American Payment Systems, United States, 2022 to 2025.** The
Federal Trade Commission said this card processor took money from small
businesses' accounts without consent and tied them to contracts with
hidden terms and exit fees. The company agreed to return $4.9 million. By
February 2025, cheques totalling more than $2.6 million had gone to 5,588
businesses, about $465 each (worked out here).[^fapss]

## What these cases say

They do not say that banks or payment companies are bad, or that any of
this will happen to a given shop. They say that money held by someone
else comes with that party's rules, its failures and its country's
emergencies, and that a business can wait days, months or years through
no fault of its own.

## Why bitcoin in the shop's own wallet is different

Every case above needed someone between the business and its money: a
processor that could hold a payout, a bank that could close an account or
fail, a government that could cap what came out of the banks. Bitcoin in
the shop's own wallet has no one in that place. It is not a balance in an
account; it sits on a network that no single company or government runs,
and it moves only when the shop's own keys sign for it. Nobody can put a
reserve on it, close the account or cap the withdrawals, because there is
no account, and no processor can hold a payout, because there is no
payout.

This holds only while the bitcoin stays in the shop's own wallet. Bitcoin
left with an exchange or a payment company is again money held by someone
else, and changing bitcoin into local money brings a bank back in at that
moment.

## Why the keys need so much care

With nobody in the middle, nobody can undo a mistake either. There is no
fraud desk, no password reset and no court that can move the coins back.
Whoever has the shop's 12 words can spend its money, and if every copy of
the words is lost, the money stays where it is for good.

That is why OpenBMT asks for more care at setup than a bank account does.
The words are made where nobody else can see them, written by hand, never
typed into a phone or a connected computer, and put on steel as soon as
possible.

## Why a coin or a die

Every wallet starts from one large random number, and the 12 words are
that number written out. Phones, computers and hardware wallets make the
number with chips and software, and those have had faults before: a number
that looked random was one that someone who knew the fault could work out.

A coin or a die has no chip, no software and no update. Nothing can reach
into it over a network, and if it favours one side, the method in
[Setting up a shop](setting-up.md#why-so-many-flips-and-rolls) removes the
bias. What remains is the computer that turns the flips or rolls into
words, and OpenBMT keeps that step as small and as checkable as it can.
The key maker page runs on Tails with the network off, shows its working,
and gives commands to check the result by hand, and Electrum, made by
other people, must show the same wallet before the shop uses it.
