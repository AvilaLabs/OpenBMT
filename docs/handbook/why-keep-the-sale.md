# You keep the sale

A card sale is not finished when the customer walks out. The shop waits
to be paid, pays a share to everyone in the chain, and can still lose the
sale weeks later. A bitcoin sale is finished when it is confirmed. This
page puts the two side by side.

## The cut on every sale

On Square's Free plan in the United States, a card tapped at the counter
costs the shop 2.6% plus 15 cents. On a $20 sale that is 67 cents. At a
thousand such sales a month it is $670 a month, $8,040 a year. A card
keyed in costs 3.5% plus 15 cents, and a card from abroad another
1.5%.[^square]

The cut is shared out along the chain. The customer's bank alone took an
average of 34 cents on a US debit card sale in 2024, 0.73% of the sale,
before the network and the processor added theirs.[^fed-debit] Small shops
pay more than large ones for the same service: in Australia, a small
merchant on a single-rate plan pays 1.4% of each card sale on average, and
a large merchant 0.6%.[^rba] When the UK and the EU capped what card
issuers may charge, what small merchants paid in all barely moved: the
issuers' share fell by about 0.15 to 0.19 points, and the small
merchants' total charge by 0.03 points or less.[^psr] The saving stayed in
the middle.

A bitcoin payment has no percentage. The customer pays the network a fee
to send it, as a sender pays postage. The shop pays a network fee later,
when it moves what it received, and that fee depends on the size of the
transaction, not on the price of what was sold. OpenBMT takes nothing.
[Costs and cashing out](why-bitcoin.md) sets out every fee, including
when many small payments cost more to move than they are worth.

## A paid sale stays paid

In 2025, $34 billion of card payments were disputed by cardholders and
charged back to merchants.[^javelin] A dispute can take two to three
months. The disputed money is held for all of that time, and the outcome
is decided by the customer's bank, not by the processor and not by the
shop.[^stripe-disputes]

A confirmed bitcoin payment cannot be pulled back by the customer, the
customer's bank or anyone else. A refund is a new payment that the shop
decides to make. That puts the shop's own refund policy in place of the
bank's decision, and consumer law still applies to the shop.

## Paid now, not when released

A new Stripe account waits 7 to 14 days for its first payout, depending
on its industry, country and risk level, and after that every sale
reaches the bank days after it was made.[^stripe-payouts] On weekends and
holidays the wait is longer.

A bitcoin payment is in the shop's wallet as soon as it is confirmed,
about ten minutes on average, on any day and at any hour. Nobody
releases it, because nobody is holding it.

## Nothing to steal at the till

A card payment carries the customer's card number through the shop's
equipment, and the shop is made responsible for guarding it. Small shops
are a favourite target: the body that sets the card security rules cites
Verizon's breach report that 61% of the breached organisations surveyed
were small businesses.[^pci]

A bitcoin payment carries no card number, no name and no account. Nothing
the shop holds would let anyone take more money from the customer.

Cash has its own costs. In Finland, a retailer paid on average €1,293 in
2024 to have cash supplied, collected and deposited, before counting the
staff time or any theft.[^bof]

## A processor can take bitcoin too

Square now lets US sellers take bitcoin, with no fee until 31 December
2026 and 1% after that, and changes it into dollars if the
seller chooses. It is not offered in New York.[^square-btc] That brings
the middle back: the money lands in Square's hands, under Square's terms,
like any other Square payment. The difference OpenBMT makes is not that
the customer pays in bitcoin. It is that the bitcoin goes to the shop.
